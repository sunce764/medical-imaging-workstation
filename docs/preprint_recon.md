## Abstract

Reducing radiation dose in X-ray computed tomography (CT) means acquiring fewer or noisier projections, which degrades reconstruction quality. The tradeoffs involved are well established in the CT literature, yet they are rarely demonstrated end-to-end through a single, openly reproducible pipeline. We present such a study on the Shepp-Logan phantom, using the same reconstruction code that ships inside an interactive teaching workstation. We quantify three effects. First, filtered back-projection (FBP) error decreases monotonically with the number of projection views and saturates beyond ≈180 views (RMSE 0.222→0.036; SSIM 0.35→0.95), giving an explicit "diminishing-returns" operating point. Second, the *optimal* apodisation filter inverts with dose: at sparse angles a smoothing (Hann) filter minimises error, while at dense angles the sharp Ram-Lak filter wins, with a crossover near 45–60 views. Third, under a Beer–Lambert + Poisson photon-noise model, a constrained iterative solver (ART with a non-negativity constraint) is the most robust across doses, whereas an unregularised least-squares inversion becomes catastrophically unstable near the square-system regime (RMSE spiking to 0.611 at 60 views for a 64×64 image), consistent with a condition-number argument. All figures are regenerated by two scripts and use no patient data. The contribution is not a new algorithm but a compact, honest, reproducible characterisation suitable for teaching and as a baseline.

**Keywords:** computed tomography, sparse-view CT, low-dose CT, filtered back-projection, iterative reconstruction, reproducibility.

## 1. Introduction

CT radiation dose is a clinical concern, and dose reduction is achieved primarily by (i) acquiring fewer projection angles (sparse-view CT) or (ii) lowering the tube current, which reduces the detected photon count and raises noise (low-dose CT) [1]. Both degrade the reconstruction, and the appropriate reconstruction strategy differs between the two regimes. Analytic methods such as filtered back-projection (FBP) are fast and standard but assume dense, low-noise sampling; iterative and model-based methods trade computation for robustness under sparse or noisy data [2, 3].

These tradeoffs are textbook material [4]. What is comparatively uncommon is a *single, self-contained, reproducible* demonstration that measures them end-to-end — from forward projection to metric — through one open code path, rather than reporting each effect from a different toolbox with different conventions. This manuscript provides that. Concretely, we answer three questions on a common phantom and pipeline:

1. **How many views are enough?** How do reconstruction error and structural similarity scale with projection count for FBP?
2. **Does the best filter depend on dose?** How do five standard FBP apodisation filters rank across sparse and dense sampling?
3. **Analytic or iterative under noise?** How do FBP, direct least-squares inversion, and the iterative solvers ART and SIRT compare under realistic photon noise?

Our aim is pedagogical and baseline-oriented: the value is in reproducibility and clarity, not novelty. The exact algorithms studied are the ones exposed to users by an interactive CT teaching workstation, so the measurements characterise a real, shipped implementation.

## 2. Background and Related Work

**Filtered back-projection.** FBP inverts the Radon transform by ramp-filtering each projection and back-projecting. The ramp (Ram-Lak) filter is exact in the continuous, noise-free limit but amplifies high-frequency noise; apodisation windows (Shepp-Logan, cosine, Hamming, Hann) taper the ramp at high frequencies to suppress noise at the cost of spatial resolution [4]. The choice of window is therefore a bias–variance tradeoff whose optimum should depend on the noise level — and hence on dose.

**Iterative reconstruction.** Algebraic methods solve the discretised system `A x = p`, where `A` is the system (projection) matrix and `p` the measured sinogram. ART (the Kaczmarz row-action method) [5] updates one ray at a time; SIRT applies all rays simultaneously with normalisation, trading per-iteration cost for smoother, noise-robust convergence. Adding a non-negativity constraint acts as a mild regulariser. In contrast, solving the same system by unconstrained least squares (here called Direct Matrix Reconstruction, DMR) has no such regularisation and is sensitive to the conditioning of `A`; near the determined regime (number of equations ≈ number of unknowns) the condition number peaks and noise is maximally amplified [2]. Modern model-based iterative reconstruction (MBIR) extends these ideas with statistical weighting and edge-preserving priors [3]; we deliberately restrict ourselves to the classical solvers to keep the study transparent and fully reproducible.

**Phantom and metrics.** The Shepp-Logan head phantom [6] is the canonical analytic CT test object. We report root-mean-square error (RMSE), normalised RMSE, structural similarity (SSIM) and peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) computed within the reconstructed field of view.

## 3. Methods

**Test object.** The Shepp-Logan phantom is rescaled to the working resolution, normalised to `[0,1]`, and masked to the inscribed circle so that it matches the support of `radon(circle=True)`. The masked phantom is the ground truth, because the sinogram encodes information only inside that circle; comparisons are therefore restricted to the in-circle region.

**Forward model.** Projections are computed with the Radon transform over a fixed 180° arc. The **dose proxy** is the number of projection angles `n_θ`: fewer angles approximate lower dose (fewer acquisitions). For the analytic-vs-iterative comparison we additionally corrupt the sinogram with a **Beer–Lambert + Poisson photon-noise model**: for incident photon count `I₀`, transmitted counts are `N ~ Poisson(I₀ · e^{−p})` and the noisy projection is `p′ = −ln(N/I₀)`; smaller `I₀` means lower dose and higher noise. A fixed random seed makes the noise reproducible.

**Reconstruction methods.** (a) FBP with five filters — ramp/Ram-Lak, Shepp-Logan, cosine, Hamming, Hann. (b) DMR: unconstrained least-squares solution of `A x = p` (`numpy.linalg.lstsq`). (c) ART: Kaczmarz iteration with a per-iteration non-negativity projection. (d) SIRT: simultaneous update with row/column normalisation and non-negativity. The system matrix `A` is built column-by-column via the Radon transform of unit-pixel images.

**Metrics.** In-circle RMSE and normalised RMSE, SSIM (data range 1.0), and PSNR. Experiments A and B use a 256×256 phantom (FBP is fast); the matrix-based methods (C) use 64×64 to keep `A` tractable, with ART run for 5 sweeps and SIRT for 100 iterations.

**Implementation.** Python 3.10 with NumPy, SciPy, and scikit-image (`radon`/`iradon`). The reconstruction routines are those used by the workstation's reconstruction laboratory; the study scripts import them directly. Full commands appear in the Reproducibility section.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Dose–quality curve (FBP, Ram-Lak, 256×256)

Table 1 and Figure 1 show FBP quality versus projection count. RMSE falls monotonically from 0.222 at 15 views to 0.036 at 360 views; SSIM rises from 0.35 to 0.95. The curve is steep up to ≈90 views and flattens thereafter: going from 15→90 views cuts RMSE by 80%, whereas 180→360 views improves it by <3%. Beyond ≈180 views additional dose buys little quality — an explicit diminishing-returns operating point.

### 4.2 The optimal filter inverts with dose (256×256)

Table 2 and Figure 2 rank five FBP filters by RMSE across projection counts. There is no globally best filter. At 20 views the smoothing **Hann** filter is best (0.143 vs 0.176 for Ram-Lak); by 180 views the sharp **Ram-Lak** is best (0.037 vs 0.055 for Hann). The ranking crosses over near 45–60 views. This is the expected bias–variance behaviour: at sparse angles, high-frequency streak noise dominates and apodisation (which attenuates high frequencies) reduces error; as sampling becomes dense and noise falls, resolution dominates and the un-tapered ramp wins.

### 4.3 Analytic vs iterative under photon noise (64×64, I₀=3×10⁴)

Table 3 and Figure 3 compare the four reconstructors under Poisson photon noise. **ART attains the lowest RMSE at every dose** — its non-negativity constraint and row-action updates act as an implicit regulariser. **DMR (unconstrained least squares) is unstable:** at 60 views its RMSE spikes to 0.611, roughly an order of magnitude worse than the others. With 64 detectors × 60 views the system has ≈3,840 equations for 4,096 unknowns — nearly square — so its condition number is worst there and photon noise is maximally amplified; the 30-view (under-determined, minimum-norm) and 90-view (over-determined, averaging) cases are far more stable. **SIRT is stable but conservative** (≈0.075), more robust than DMR and slightly behind ART. FBP is a stable but streak-limited baseline (≈0.09).

| Views | FBP | DMR (least-squares) | ART | SIRT |
|------:|----:|----:|----:|----:|
| 30 | 0.099 | 0.151 | **0.069** | 0.083 |
| 60 | 0.089 | **0.611** | **0.053** | 0.076 |
| 90 | 0.087 | 0.090 | **0.050** | 0.075 |

*Table 3. In-circle RMSE under photon noise. Bold marks the best method (ART) and the catastrophic DMR failure at the near-square regime.*

## 5. Discussion

The three results form one coherent message: **the right reconstruction choice depends on the dose regime.** In the abundant-dose regime, FBP with the sharp Ram-Lak filter is accurate and fast, and extra views add little. As dose falls — whether by sparser sampling or lower photon counts — the picture changes twice over. First, within FBP, the filter optimum shifts toward apodisation, because suppressing amplified high-frequency noise matters more than preserving resolution (§4.2). Second, when noise is genuinely photon-limited, unregularised inversion is the wrong tool: its error is governed by the conditioning of the projection matrix, which is worst exactly where the number of measurements matches the number of unknowns (§4.3). Constrained iteration (ART) side-steps this by injecting prior information (non-negativity) and never explicitly inverting `A`, which is why it dominates across the noisy doses tested. These observations reproduce, in miniature and end-to-end, the rationale behind the field's move toward iterative and model-based reconstruction for low-dose CT [3].

The condition-number explanation for the DMR spike is worth emphasising because it is a common source of confusion: a *larger* number of views (90) yields a *more* stable least-squares solution than a near-square system (60), even though both are "more data" than the sparse case. Data quantity alone does not determine stability; the geometry of the linear system does.

## 6. Limitations

This is a deliberately small, transparent study. (i) It uses a single 2-D analytic phantom; real anatomy, 3-D geometry, beam hardening, and scatter are not modelled. (ii) The matrix methods are limited to 64×64 by the cost of forming `A`, so absolute numbers in §4.3 are not directly comparable to the 256×256 FBP results in §4.1–4.2. (iii) The photon-noise model is a simplified monoenergetic Beer–Lambert model with a single `I₀`; electronic noise and bowtie filtration are omitted. (iv) ART/SIRT iteration counts were fixed rather than tuned per dose. These choices favour reproducibility and interpretability over realism; none affect the qualitative conclusions, which are consistent with established CT theory.

## 7. Conclusion

Using one open pipeline and a standard phantom, we quantified three dose-dependent reconstruction tradeoffs: FBP quality saturates beyond ≈180 views; the optimal FBP filter inverts from apodising (sparse) to sharp (dense) with a 45–60-view crossover; and under photon noise, constrained iteration (ART) is most robust while unregularised least-squares inversion destabilises near the square-system regime. The study is fully reproducible from two scripts and uses no patient data, making it suitable as a teaching artefact and as a baseline for further work on sparse-view and low-dose reconstruction.
