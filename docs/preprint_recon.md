## Abstract

Reducing radiation dose in X-ray computed tomography (CT) means acquiring fewer or noisier projections, which degrades reconstruction quality. The tradeoffs involved are well established in the CT literature, yet they are rarely demonstrated end-to-end through a single, openly reproducible pipeline. We present such a study on the Shepp-Logan phantom, using the same reconstruction code that ships inside an interactive teaching workstation. We quantify three effects. First, filtered back-projection (FBP) error decreases monotonically with the number of projection views and saturates beyond ≈180 views (RMSE 0.222→0.035; SSIM 0.35→0.95), which earlier revisions read as an explicit "diminishing-returns" operating point — a reading we then withdraw in §4.6, where the plateau is measured to be the reconstruction chain's own discretisation floor. Second, the *optimal* apodisation filter inverts with dose: at sparse angles a smoothing (Hann) filter minimises error, while at dense angles the sharp Ram-Lak filter wins, the **pairwise Ram-Lak-versus-Hann** crossover falling near 45–60 views and Ram-Lak becoming the best of all five only between 60 and 90 (§4.2). Third, under a Beer–Lambert + Poisson photon-noise model, a constrained iterative solver (ART) reaches the lowest error at the iteration counts we fixed — a ranking we then withdraw in §4.5, where sweeping the stopping point reverses it in SIRT's favour at every dose, whereas an unregularised least-squares inversion becomes catastrophically unstable near the square-system regime (RMSE spiking to 0.611 at 60 views for a 64×64 image). We then measure the conditioning that is usually invoked to explain this. The 2-norm condition number is the wrong instrument — the sparse and near-square systems are both rank-deficient, so it is infinite for both, and the finite values printed for it are rounding artefacts of the SVD, not properties of `A`. Stated instead about the spectrum least squares actually retains, the account is supported and sharp: ‖A⁺‖₂ peaks at the near-square point at 1,076 against 29.5 and 46.9, while the implicit truncated-SVD regularisation that stabilises the sparse case collapses monotonically (2,177 discarded directions against 257 and 0). We report that agreement and explicitly decline to turn it into a quantitative attribution, which the measurements do not support. A TV-regularised solver (ASD-POCS), added because the original method set omitted the standard sparse-view baseline, cuts the error of the best of these by 45–55% at this study's noise level — but the advantage is monotone in SNR and is gone by η≈9%, where it trails at 60 and 90 views and leads only at 30, where its structural similarity is also the worse of the two, so the reportable result is the dependence and not the headline (§4.7). All figures and tables are regenerated by three scripts, and use no patient data — this study is phantom-only. The contribution is not a new algorithm but a compact, honest, reproducible characterisation suitable for teaching and as a baseline.

**Keywords:** computed tomography, sparse-view CT, low-dose CT, filtered back-projection, iterative reconstruction, reproducibility.

## 1. Introduction

CT radiation dose is a clinical concern, and dose reduction is achieved primarily by (i) acquiring fewer projection angles (sparse-view CT) or (ii) lowering the tube current, which reduces the detected photon count and raises noise (low-dose CT) [1]. Both degrade the reconstruction, and the appropriate reconstruction strategy differs between the two regimes. Analytic methods such as filtered back-projection (FBP) are fast and standard but assume dense, low-noise sampling; iterative and model-based methods trade computation for robustness under sparse or noisy data [2, 3].

These tradeoffs are textbook material [4]. What is comparatively uncommon is a *single, self-contained, reproducible* demonstration that measures them end-to-end — from forward projection to metric — through one open code path, rather than reporting each effect from a different toolbox with different conventions. This manuscript provides that. Concretely, we answer three questions on a common phantom and pipeline:

1. **How many views are enough?** How do reconstruction error and structural similarity scale with projection count for FBP?
2. **Does the best filter depend on dose?** How do five standard FBP apodisation filters rank across sparse and dense sampling?
3. **Analytic or iterative under noise?** How do FBP, direct least-squares inversion, and the iterative solvers ART and SIRT compare under a simplified monoenergetic Beer–Lambert + Poisson photon-noise model?

Our aim is pedagogical and baseline-oriented: the value is in reproducibility and clarity, not novelty. The experiments call the workstation's shipped numerical module directly. BP, FBP, DFR, DMR, ART, SIRT and ASD-POCS are all exposed through the GUI, so every solver measured here is one a user can run. ASD-POCS carries a method-specific iteration list in that GUI: at the 10–20 sweeps that suit ART/SIRT it is measurably worse than FBP (§4.7), so offering it those values would misrepresent a correct implementation.

## 2. Background and Related Work

**Filtered back-projection.** FBP inverts the Radon transform by ramp-filtering each projection and back-projecting. The ramp (Ram-Lak) filter is exact in the continuous, noise-free limit but amplifies high-frequency noise; apodisation windows (Shepp-Logan, cosine, Hamming, Hann) taper the ramp at high frequencies to suppress noise at the cost of spatial resolution [4]. The choice of window is therefore a bias–variance tradeoff whose optimum should depend on the noise level — and hence on dose.

**Iterative reconstruction.** Algebraic methods solve the discretised system `A x = p`, where `A` is the system (projection) matrix and `p` the measured sinogram. ART (the Kaczmarz row-action method) [5] updates one ray at a time; SIRT applies all rays simultaneously with normalisation, trading per-iteration cost for smoother, noise-robust convergence. Adding a non-negativity constraint acts as a mild regulariser. In contrast, solving the same system by unconstrained least squares (here called Direct Matrix Reconstruction, DMR) has no such regularisation and is sensitive to the conditioning of `A`; the textbook expectation is that near the determined regime (number of equations ≈ number of unknowns) the condition number peaks and noise is maximally amplified [2]. We treat that expectation as a hypothesis to be measured rather than an explanation to be assumed, and test it directly in §4.4. Modern model-based iterative reconstruction (MBIR) extends these ideas with statistical weighting and edge-preserving priors [3]; we deliberately restrict ourselves to the classical solvers to keep the study transparent and fully reproducible.

**Phantom and metrics.** The Shepp-Logan head phantom [6] is the canonical analytic CT test object. We report root-mean-square error (RMSE), normalised RMSE, structural similarity (SSIM) and peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) computed within the reconstructed field of view.

## 3. Methods

**Test object.** The Shepp-Logan phantom is rescaled to the working resolution, normalised to `[0,1]`, and masked to the inscribed circle so that it matches the support of `radon(circle=True)`. The masked phantom is the ground truth, because the sinogram encodes information only inside that circle; comparisons are therefore restricted to the in-circle region.

**Forward model.** Projections are computed with the Radon transform over a fixed 180° arc. The **dose proxy** is the number of projection angles `n_θ`: fewer angles approximate lower dose (fewer acquisitions). For the analytic-vs-iterative comparison we additionally corrupt the sinogram with a **Beer–Lambert + Poisson photon-noise model**: for incident photon count `I₀`, transmitted counts are `N ~ Poisson(I₀ · e^{−p})` and the noisy projection is `p′ = −ln(N/I₀)`; smaller `I₀` means lower dose and higher noise. A fixed random seed makes the noise reproducible.

**Reconstruction methods.** (a) FBP with five filters — ramp/Ram-Lak, Shepp-Logan, cosine, Hamming, Hann. (b) DMR: unconstrained least-squares solution of `A x = p` (`numpy.linalg.lstsq`). (c) ART: Kaczmarz iteration with a per-iteration non-negativity projection. (d) SIRT: simultaneous update with row/column normalisation and non-negativity. The system matrix `A` is built column-by-column via the Radon transform of unit-pixel images.

**Metrics.** In-circle RMSE and normalised RMSE, SSIM (data range 1.0), and PSNR. Experiments A and B use a 256×256 phantom (FBP is fast); the matrix-based methods (C) use 64×64 to keep `A` tractable, with ART run for 5 sweeps and SIRT for 100 iterations.

**Implementation.** Python 3.10 with NumPy, SciPy, and scikit-image (`radon`/`iradon`). The reconstruction routines are those used by the workstation's reconstruction laboratory; the study scripts import them directly. Full commands appear in the Reproducibility section.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Dose–quality curve (FBP, Ram-Lak, 256×256)

Table 1 and Figure 1 show FBP quality versus projection count. RMSE falls monotonically from 0.222 at 15 views to 0.035 at 360 views; SSIM rises from 0.35 to 0.95. The curve is steep up to ≈90 views and flattens thereafter: going from 15→90 views cuts RMSE by 80%, whereas 180→360 views improves it by only 3.2%. Beyond ≈180 views additional dose buys little further error reduction, while SSIM continues to climb slowly (0.90→0.95).

**What the flattening is and is not.** Earlier revisions read this as an explicit "diminishing returns" operating point for dose. §4.6 measures it and finds the plateau is a **metric floor of the reconstruction implementation** (in-circle RMSE stops at 0.03539 and does not move from 720 to 2,880 views), not a statement that the dose is sufficient. The *location* of the knee near ≈180–256 views does agree with the Kak–Slaney sampling criterion; the *height* of the plateau does not carry dose meaning.

### 4.2 The optimal filter inverts with dose (256×256)

Table 2 and Figure 2 rank five FBP filters by RMSE across projection counts. There is no globally best filter. At 20 views the smoothing **Hann** filter is best (0.143 vs 0.176 for Ram-Lak); by 180 views the sharp **Ram-Lak** is best (0.037 vs 0.055 for Hann). The **pairwise Ram-Lak–Hann** ranking crosses over near 45–60 views. Across all five filters the optimum passes through Hamming at 45 and Shepp-Logan at 60, so Ram-Lak becomes the global best only between 60 and 90 views (Table 2). This is the expected bias–variance behaviour: at sparse angles, high-frequency streak noise dominates and apodisation (which attenuates high frequencies) reduces error; as sampling becomes dense and noise falls, resolution dominates and the un-tapered ramp wins.

### 4.3 Analytic vs iterative under photon noise (64×64, I₀=3×10⁴)

Table 3 and Figure 3 compare the four reconstructors under Poisson photon noise. **ART attains the lowest RMSE at every dose _at the iteration counts used here_ (5 sweeps versus SIRT's 100)** — but that ranking does not survive a fair stopping rule, and the causal reading this paper previously attached to it is withdrawn in §4.5. **DMR (unconstrained least squares) is unstable:** at 60 views its RMSE spikes to 0.611, roughly an order of magnitude worse than the others. With 64 detectors × 60 views the system has 3,840 equations for 4,096 unknowns — nearly square — while the 30-view (under-determined, minimum-norm) and 90-view (over-determined) cases are far more stable. The standard reading is that conditioning is worst at the near-square point; §4.4 measures it, and finds the reading correct in substance but not expressible through the 2-norm condition number, which is undefined for two of these three systems. **SIRT is stable but conservative** (≈0.075), more robust than DMR and slightly behind ART. FBP is a stable but streak-limited baseline (≈0.09).

### 4.4 Measuring the conditioning that §4.3 only asserted

The account above is the standard one, and until now it was the *only* thing in this study that had never been measured. To close that gap we compute the singular value decomposition of the very same system matrices §4.3 uses — `recon_cond.py` calls `recon.make_theta` and `recon.build_system_matrix` on the identical arguments, so the matrices are bit-for-bit the ones the reconstructions ran on.

| Views | Equations × unknowns | cond₂(A) | Numerical rank | Retained by `lstsq` | σ at cutoff | Noise gain 1/σ_k | Discarded directions | DMR RMSE |
|------:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 30 | 1,920 × 4,096 | ∞ (rank-deficient) | 1,919 | 1,919 | 0.0340 | 29.5 | 2,177 | 0.151 |
| 60 | 3,840 × 4,096 | ∞ (rank-deficient) | 3,839 | 3,839 | 9.30 × 10⁻⁴ | **1,076** | 257 | **0.611** |
| 90 | 5,760 × 4,096 | 3.36 × 10³ | 4,096 | 4,096 | 0.0213 | 46.9 | 0 | 0.090 |

*Table 4. Measured conditioning of the §4.3 system matrices. The retained rank is not computed here but read back from `numpy.linalg.lstsq(A, p, rcond=None)` itself — the same call `compute_dmr` makes — so the two cannot drift apart. Reproduce with `python experiments/recon_cond.py` → `results/exp_c_conditioning.csv`.*

Two things follow, and a third is explicitly withheld.

**cond₂ is the wrong instrument for two of the three systems, so the textbook claim is inapplicable rather than false.** At 30 and 60 views `A` is rank-deficient — σ_min is 2.8 × 10⁻¹⁶ and 7.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ against a numerical-zero threshold of `max(m,n)·ε·σ_max` ≈ 3.8 × 10⁻¹¹ and 5.3 × 10⁻¹¹ — so cond₂ is mathematically infinite for both and any finite value printed for it is rounding noise. That this is noise rather than signal follows from the threshold itself: σ_min sits five orders of magnitude below the numerical-zero cutoff, so any `σ_max/σ_min` printed for these two systems reports the rounding floor of the SVD, not a property of `A`. `recon_cond.py` accordingly records `cond₂` as `inf` for both. **A row-permutation control was never scripted or committed, so no measured before/after pair is claimed here.** Only the 90-view system, which is full rank, has a cond₂ worth quoting.

**Stated about the spectrum least squares actually retains, the conditioning account is supported, and sharply.** `lstsq` discards singular directions below its cutoff, so the operative gain on the worst retained direction is `‖A⁺‖₂ = 1/σ_k`, and that goes 29.5 → **1,076** → 46.9: a factor of 36.5 above the sparse case and 22.9 above the dense one, peaking exactly at the near-square regime where the RMSE spikes. The mechanism the textbook describes is real here; what fails is only the attempt to express it through σ_min. The count of discarded directions moves monotonically over the same range (2,177 → 257 → 0), which is the other half of the picture: at 30 views more than half the solution space is projected away, and that implicit truncated-SVD regularisation is what keeps the sparse case stable despite being the most rank-deficient of the three.

**What this does *not* license is any quantitative attribution of the spike.** An earlier revision of this section divided the RMSE ratios by the `1/σ_k` ratios and reported the remainder as "a factor of ~3.5 unexplained". That arithmetic is invalid and is withdrawn. `‖A⁺‖₂` is a worst-case operator-norm bound on the amplification of a perturbation aligned with one singular direction; the reconstruction RMSE is an end-to-end quantity in which the noise is Poisson (so its covariance changes with view count, and with the sinogram values themselves), the discarded directions contribute truncation *bias* rather than amplified variance, and the solution is clipped to `[0, 1]` before the error is taken in-circle — a step that compresses precisely the largest excursions, and therefore bites hardest at the 60-view point this section is about. A ratio of the bound and a ratio of the outcome are not commensurable, and their quotient is not a residual. Decomposing the spike properly would need the noise covariance propagated through `A⁺` — for instance `Σᵢ (uᵢᵀδp)²/σᵢ²` with the actual per-ray variances — plus a separate accounting of truncation bias and clipping. **That was not done here, so this study reports the measured facts above and makes no claim about how much of the 0.611 either one explains.**

**Erratum.** The first version of this section reported the retained rank from a hand-written cutoff of `max(m,n)·ε_float32·σ_max`, on the reasoning that `A` is stored as float32. That is wrong: `numpy.linalg.lstsq` upcasts unconditionally to double (`_commonType` in `numpy/linalg/_linalg.py` returns `double` for every real input), so the cutoff uses ε_float64 and is eight orders of magnitude smaller. The error understated the 60-view noise gain as 34.6 instead of 1,076 and led this section to report that the textbook expectation had been refuted, when the corrected measurement supports it. The tell was visible in the table and was missed: the 90-view row simultaneously read "full rank" and "15 discarded directions", which cannot both be true. `recon_cond.py` no longer reimplements the cutoff; it reads the rank back from the same `lstsq` call under study.

### 4.5 The ART-versus-SIRT ranking is a stopping rule, not a property

§4.3 fixes ART at 5 sweeps and SIRT at 100 iterations. Those numbers are hardcoded, with no convergence check and no stopping criterion, and the paper drew a causal conclusion from the ranking they produce. Sweeping both solvers over the same phantom, noise realisation and system matrix (`experiments/recon_stopping.py` → `results/exp_c_stopping.csv`) shows the ranking is an artefact of that choice.

| Views | ART best (sweeps) | SIRT best (iterations) | SIRT better by |
|------:|---:|---:|---:|
| 30 | 0.04407 @ 100 | **0.03998** @ 3200 | 9.3% |
| 60 | 0.02711 @ 35 | **0.02312** @ 3200 | 14.7% |
| 90 | 0.02084 @ 20 | **0.01674** @ 3200 | 19.7% |

*Table 5. Best in-circle RMSE reached by each solver over the swept iteration grid, at the noise realisation of Table 3. Both bounds are grid-limited: SIRT is still improving at 3,200 iterations, and ART's 30-view optimum sits at the grid edge, so neither optimum is bracketed.*

Three corrections follow.

**The comparison in §4.3 is not equal-compute.** The conventional unit is the projection pair: one ART sweep and one SIRT iteration each cost about one forward- plus one back-projection. Five sweeps against a hundred iterations is therefore a **1:20 compute ratio** — SIRT was losing while being given twenty times the budget.

**At their own optima the ranking reverses at every dose**, by 9–20%. The defensible statement is that **ART converges far faster while SIRT reaches a lower floor**; "ART is the most robust solver" is withdrawn, and with it the §5 claim that its non-negativity constraint and row-action updates explain a robustness advantage. That explanation was never tested — no relaxation parameter was varied, no ordering was varied, and no stopping criterion was applied.

**Semi-convergence does not appear anywhere in the swept range, and that is a property of the noise level, not of the solvers.** The measured relative noise is **η ≈ 0.9%** at all three doses. Row-action and simultaneous solvers are expected to show error falling and then rising as they approach the noisy least-squares solution; at this noise level the turning point lies beyond 3,200 iterations. Reporting "no semi-convergence was observed" without that qualifier would misdescribe a well-known property as absent.

### 4.6 Two further limits of the setup, measured rather than assumed

**The saturation in §4.1 is a metric floor, not a dose conclusion.** §4.1 reports that error saturates beyond ≈180 views, and earlier revisions read that as an "enough is enough" operating point for dose. Pushing the noise-free sweep far past any sampling criterion (`experiments/recon_floor.py` → `results/exp_a_metric_floor.csv`) shows in-circle RMSE bottoming out at **≈0.03539**: 720, 1,440 and 2,880 views give 0.035394, 0.035386 and 0.035388, agreeing to within **0.023%** and no longer descending — the last point is marginally *above* the one before it, which is what a floor plus interpolation noise looks like, not continued improvement. A fourfold increase in views buys nothing. At 180 views the error is already only **3.6%** above that floor. The plateau height is set by the discretisation error of the reconstruction chain (spline `zoom` plus `iradon`'s ramp filtering and interpolation), not by dose. SSIM saturates too, at 0.9578 by 1,440 views, but later than RMSE — which is what a metric floor looks like, since the two metrics hit their own floors at different points. The knee does sit near the Kak–Slaney criterion (projections ≈ rays per projection, i.e. ≈256 here), so the *location* of the knee is meaningful; the *height* of the plateau is not a statement about dose.

**Study I commits an inverse crime, and it is not a mild one.** `experiments/recon_study.py` generates every sinogram with `skimage.transform.radon`, and `recon.build_system_matrix` builds `A` by calling that same `radon` on unit impulses, one column per pixel. `A` is therefore not an approximation of the forward operator — it is its exact matrix representation, as this project's own regression suite pins (`max|A·x − compute_sinogram(x)| < 1 × 10⁻⁴`; the value recorded alongside that assertion is 9.5 × 10⁻⁷, i.e. float32 rounding). DMR, ART and SIRT are inverting precisely the operator that produced the data, with zero model error; FBP goes through `iradon` and does not use `A` at all. The consequence is worse than a uniformly optimistic result: **the iterative solvers commit the crime and the analytic baseline does not**, so the analytic-versus-iterative comparison that §4.3 is built on carries a systematic bias in favour of the matrix methods. Standard mitigations are to project on a finer grid and downsample, to use the closed-form Radon transform of the Shepp-Logan ellipses, or to use different projectors for the forward and inverse steps. None was applied here, and the magnitude of the resulting optimism has not been measured, so §4.3's cross-family comparison should be read as indicative only.
### 4.7 A TV-regularised baseline, and the SNR range over which it helps

and sweeps it against the same phantom, noise realisations and system matrices used in
§4.5, with all solvers oracle-stopped (`results/exp_c_asdpocs.csv`).

| η (measured) | 30 views | 60 views | 90 views | SSIM @ 90 views |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ≈0.9% | +54.7% | +48.8% | +45.1% | 0.948 → 0.979 |

At this study's operating point (η≈0.9%) ASD-POCS reaches 0.0092–0.0181 against SIRT's
floor of 0.0167–0.0400 — roughly half the error of the best solver §4.5 identified.
**The result we report, however, is the dependence rather than the headline number.**
The advantage is monotone in SNR and inverts: by η≈9% ASD-POCS loses at 60 and 90 views,
and where the two nearly tie on RMSE the structural metric separates them in the opposite
direction (SSIM 0.519 against 0.687 at 90 views), which is TV staircasing. A study
reporting only the −50% would state the low-dose regime backwards, so both metrics are
given at every point.

Shepp-Logan is piecewise constant and therefore the ideal object for a TV prior, which
makes the whole table suspect. Repeating it on a TV-adversarial phantom — Shepp-Logan
plus a smoothed random field that breaks piecewise constancy — leaves it intact
(+56.4% / +56.4% / +45.7% at η≈0.9%, with the same reversal by η≈9%). That control was
run to deflate the result and did not.

Two limits travel with the numbers. `n_iter` does not transfer from this repository's
other solvers: chosen as 20 by analogy with ART=5 / SIRT=100, ASD-POCS is *worse than
FBP* (0.106 against 0.088 at 60 views); the optima lie at 50–300, and 2 of 18 rows have
their optimum at the swept grid's edge, so those are lower bounds rather than bracketed
optima. And **the inverse crime of §4.6 bears on this result more heavily than on any
other in the paper**: ASD-POCS is a matrix method inverting the exact operator that
generated the data, while the FBP column is not — the comparison against FBP is therefore
biased in ASD-POCS's favour by an unquantified amount.

## 5. Discussion

The three results form one coherent message: **the right reconstruction choice depends on the dose regime.** In the abundant-dose regime, FBP with the sharp Ram-Lak filter is accurate and fast, and extra views add little. As dose falls — whether by sparser sampling or lower photon counts — the picture changes twice over. First, within FBP, the filter optimum shifts toward apodisation, because suppressing amplified high-frequency noise matters more than preserving resolution (§4.2). Second, when noise is genuinely photon-limited, unregularised inversion is the wrong tool: its error is governed by the conditioning of the projection matrix, and §4.4 measures that this is so — the noise gain on the retained spectrum peaks by a factor of 23–37 at exactly the near-square regime, while the implicit truncated-SVD regularisation that keeps the sparse case stable (2,177 discarded directions against 257 and 0) collapses over the same range (§4.4). Constrained iteration side-steps this by injecting prior information (non-negativity) and never explicitly inverting `A`. We previously added that this is "why ART dominates across the noisy doses tested"; that clause is withdrawn — §4.5 shows the ART-over-SIRT ranking was produced by a 1:20 compute imbalance, and reverses once each solver is allowed its own optimum. These observations reproduce, in miniature and end-to-end, the rationale behind the field's move toward iterative and model-based reconstruction for low-dose CT [3].

The DMR spike is worth dwelling on because it is a common source of confusion, and because measuring it changed our own explanation. The robust part of the observation is that a *larger* number of views (90) yields a *more* stable least-squares solution than a near-square system (60), even though both are "more data" than the sparse case: data quantity alone does not determine stability, the geometry of the linear system does. The part we had wrong is which number expresses that geometry. It is not σ_min — by that measure two of the three systems are simply singular, and the finite condition numbers printed for them are artefacts — but the smallest singular value the solver actually keeps. That distinction only became visible once the SVD was computed rather than cited. How *much* of the spike that accounts for is a separate question this study does not answer: it would require propagating the Poisson noise covariance through `A⁺` and separating truncation bias and clipping from amplified variance, none of which is done here (§4.4).

## 6. Limitations

This is a deliberately small, transparent study. (i) It uses a single 2-D analytic phantom; real anatomy, 3-D geometry, beam hardening, and scatter are not modelled. (ii) The matrix methods are limited to 64×64 by the cost of forming `A`, so absolute numbers in §4.3 are not directly comparable to the 256×256 FBP results in §4.1–4.2. (iii) The photon-noise model is a simplified monoenergetic Beer–Lambert model with a single `I₀`; electronic noise and bowtie filtration are omitted, so it should not be read as realistic dose simulation. (iv) The TV baseline of §4.7 is swept over one parameter (`n_iter`) only: α, N_grad and the relaxation schedule are held at their published values, so it is a faithful reference implementation rather than a tuned competitor, and two of its eighteen optima sit at the grid edge. ART/SIRT iteration counts were likewise fixed rather than tuned per dose; §4.5 measures what that costs and withdraws the resulting ranking. Neither solver's optimum is bracketed by the swept grid, no relaxation parameter was varied (both run at λ = 1), and no stopping criterion such as the discrepancy principle was applied. (v) §4.4 measures the conditioning of the three §4.3 systems and reports a qualitative agreement, but attempts no quantitative decomposition of the DMR spike — the noise covariance is not propagated, and truncation bias and clipping are not separated from amplified variance. These choices favour reproducibility and interpretability over realism. We previously claimed here that none of them affected the qualitative conclusions; that claim is withdrawn — it was never tested, and §4.4 is a case of a qualitative conclusion that measurement did partly overturn. (vi) The study commits an inverse crime — the system matrix is the exact matrix representation of the operator that generated the data — and, because FBP does not use that matrix, the bias falls asymmetrically on the analytic-versus-iterative comparison (§4.6). (vii) The saturation reported in §4.1 reaches a metric floor set by the reconstruction implementation, so it does not by itself establish a dose operating point (§4.6).

## 7. Conclusion

Using one open pipeline and a standard phantom, we quantified three dose-dependent reconstruction tradeoffs: FBP quality saturates beyond ≈180 views; the optimal FBP filter inverts from apodising (sparse) to sharp (dense) with a 45–60-view crossover; and under photon noise, constrained iteration (ART) is most robust — under the fixed iteration counts tested, a ranking §4.5 withdraws — while a TV-regularised solver cuts the error of the best of them by 45–55% at η≈0.9% and falls behind it at 60 and 90 views by η≈9% (§4.7), while unregularised least-squares inversion destabilises near the square-system regime, for reasons that direct measurement of the system matrices supports only in part. The study regenerates from seeded scripts and uses no patient data, making it suitable as a teaching artefact and as a baseline for further work on sparse-view and low-dose reconstruction.

## Reproducibility

All figures and tables are regenerated by:

```bash
conda activate dicom_gui
pip install -r experiments/requirements-experiments.txt
python experiments/recon_study.py a b c
python experiments/recon_cond.py
python experiments/recon_stopping.py
python experiments/recon_tv.py
python experiments/recon_floor.py
```

Outputs are written to `experiments/results/` (PNG figures + CSV data). The reconstruction routines under test are in `recon.py`; the study harness is `experiments/recon_study.py`, and `experiments/recon_cond.py` produces Table 4 by taking the SVD of the same system matrices §4.3 reconstructs from (it reuses `recon.build_system_matrix`, so it hits the on-disk matrix cache rather than rebuilding). Both are deterministic: phantom and photon noise are seeded, and the conditioning measurement has no random component at all. Environment: Python 3.10, NumPy 2.2, SciPy 1.15, scikit-image 0.25.
