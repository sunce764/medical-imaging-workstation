## Abstract

Reducing radiation dose in X-ray computed tomography (CT) means acquiring fewer or noisier projections, which degrades reconstruction quality. The tradeoffs involved are well established in the CT literature, yet they are rarely demonstrated end-to-end through a single, openly reproducible pipeline. We present such a study on the Shepp-Logan phantom, using the same reconstruction code that ships inside an interactive teaching workstation. We quantify three effects. First, filtered back-projection (FBP) error decreases monotonically with the number of projection views and saturates beyond ≈180 views (RMSE 0.222→0.035; SSIM 0.35→0.95), giving an explicit "diminishing-returns" operating point. Second, the *optimal* apodisation filter inverts with dose: at sparse angles a smoothing (Hann) filter minimises error, while at dense angles the sharp Ram-Lak filter wins, with a crossover near 45–60 views. Third, under a Beer–Lambert + Poisson photon-noise model, a constrained iterative solver (ART with a non-negativity constraint) is the most robust across doses, whereas an unregularised least-squares inversion becomes catastrophically unstable near the square-system regime (RMSE spiking to 0.611 at 60 views for a 64×64 image). We then measure the conditioning that is usually invoked to explain this, and report that the usual form of the argument does not survive: the 2-norm condition number is worst at the sparsest sampling, not at the near-square point; what peaks there is the conditioning of the subspace least squares retains (‖A⁺‖₂ = 34.6 versus 29.5 and 19.3), the only measured quantity that tracks the RMSE in shape. We report that agreement and explicitly decline to turn it into a quantitative attribution, which the measurements do not support. All figures and tables are regenerated by two scripts, and use no patient data — this study is phantom-only. The contribution is not a new algorithm but a compact, honest, reproducible characterisation suitable for teaching and as a baseline.

**Keywords:** computed tomography, sparse-view CT, low-dose CT, filtered back-projection, iterative reconstruction, reproducibility.

## 1. Introduction

CT radiation dose is a clinical concern, and dose reduction is achieved primarily by (i) acquiring fewer projection angles (sparse-view CT) or (ii) lowering the tube current, which reduces the detected photon count and raises noise (low-dose CT) [1]. Both degrade the reconstruction, and the appropriate reconstruction strategy differs between the two regimes. Analytic methods such as filtered back-projection (FBP) are fast and standard but assume dense, low-noise sampling; iterative and model-based methods trade computation for robustness under sparse or noisy data [2, 3].

These tradeoffs are textbook material [4]. What is comparatively uncommon is a *single, self-contained, reproducible* demonstration that measures them end-to-end — from forward projection to metric — through one open code path, rather than reporting each effect from a different toolbox with different conventions. This manuscript provides that. Concretely, we answer three questions on a common phantom and pipeline:

1. **How many views are enough?** How do reconstruction error and structural similarity scale with projection count for FBP?
2. **Does the best filter depend on dose?** How do five standard FBP apodisation filters rank across sparse and dense sampling?
3. **Analytic or iterative under noise?** How do FBP, direct least-squares inversion, and the iterative solvers ART and SIRT compare under a simplified monoenergetic Beer–Lambert + Poisson photon-noise model?

Our aim is pedagogical and baseline-oriented: the value is in reproducibility and clarity, not novelty. The exact algorithms studied are the ones exposed to users by an interactive CT teaching workstation, so the measurements characterise a real, shipped implementation.

## 2. Background and Related Work

**Filtered back-projection.** FBP inverts the Radon transform by ramp-filtering each projection and back-projecting. The ramp (Ram-Lak) filter is exact in the continuous, noise-free limit but amplifies high-frequency noise; apodisation windows (Shepp-Logan, cosine, Hamming, Hann) taper the ramp at high frequencies to suppress noise at the cost of spatial resolution [4]. The choice of window is therefore a bias–variance tradeoff whose optimum should depend on the noise level — and hence on dose.

**Iterative reconstruction.** Algebraic methods solve the discretised system `A x = p`, where `A` is the system (projection) matrix and `p` the measured sinogram. ART (the Kaczmarz row-action method) [5] updates one ray at a time; SIRT applies all rays simultaneously with normalisation, trading per-iteration cost for smoother, noise-robust convergence. Adding a non-negativity constraint acts as a mild regulariser. In contrast, solving the same system by unconstrained least squares (here called Direct Matrix Reconstruction, DMR) has no such regularisation and is sensitive to the conditioning of `A`; the textbook expectation is that near the determined regime (number of equations ≈ number of unknowns) the condition number peaks and noise is maximally amplified [2]. We treat that expectation as a hypothesis to be measured rather than an explanation to be assumed, and test it directly in §4.4. Modern model-based iterative reconstruction (MBIR) extends these ideas with statistical weighting and edge-preserving priors [3]; we deliberately restrict ourselves to the classical solvers to keep the study transparent and fully reproducible.

**Phantom and metrics.** The Shepp-Logan head phantom [6] is the canonical analytic CT test object. We report root-mean-square error (RMSE), normalised RMSE, structural similarity (SSIM) and peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) computed within the reconstructed field of view.

## 3. Methods

**Test object.** The Shepp-Logan phantom is rescaled to the working resolution, normalised to `[0,1]`, and masked to the inscribed circle so that it matches the support of `radon(circle=True)`. The masked phantom is the ground truth, because the sinogram encodes information only inside that circle; comparisons are therefore restricted to the in-circle region.

**Forward model.** Projections are computed with the Radon transform over a fixed 180° arc. The **dose proxy** is the number of projection angles `n_θ`: fewer angles approximate lower dose (fewer acquisitions). For the analytic-vs-iterative comparison we additionally corrupt the sinogram with a **Beer–Lambert + Poisson photon-noise model**: for incident photon count `I₀`, transmitted counts are `N ~ Poisson(I₀ · e^{−p})` and the noisy projection is `p′ = −ln(N/I₀)`; smaller `I₀` means lower dose and higher noise. A fixed random seed makes the noise reproducible.

**Reconstruction methods.** (a) FBP with five filters — ramp/Ram-Lak, Shepp-Logan, cosine, Hamming, Hann. (b) DMR: unconstrained least-squares solution of `A x = p` (`numpy.linalg.lstsq`). (c) ART: Kaczmarz iteration with a per-iteration non-negativity projection. (d) SIRT: simultaneous update with row/column normalisation and non-negativity. The system matrix `A` is built column-by-column via the Radon transform of unit-pixel images.

**Metrics.** In-circle RMSE and normalised RMSE, SSIM (data range 1.0), and PSNR. Experiments A and B use a 256×256 phantom (FBP is fast); the matrix-based methods (C) use 64×64 to keep `A` tractable, with ART run for 5 sweeps and SIRT for 100 iterations.

**Implementation.** Python 3.10 with NumPy, SciPy, and scikit-image (`radon`/`iradon`). The reconstruction routines are those used by the workstation's reconstruction laboratory; the study scripts import them directly. Full commands appear in the Reproducibility section.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Dose–quality curve (FBP, Ram-Lak, 256×256)

Table 1 and Figure 1 show FBP quality versus projection count. RMSE falls monotonically from 0.222 at 15 views to 0.035 at 360 views; SSIM rises from 0.35 to 0.95. The curve is steep up to ≈90 views and flattens thereafter: going from 15→90 views cuts RMSE by 80%, whereas 180→360 views improves it by only 3.2%. Beyond ≈180 views additional dose buys little further error reduction — an explicit diminishing-returns operating point — while SSIM continues to climb slowly (0.90→0.95).

### 4.2 The optimal filter inverts with dose (256×256)

Table 2 and Figure 2 rank five FBP filters by RMSE across projection counts. There is no globally best filter. At 20 views the smoothing **Hann** filter is best (0.143 vs 0.176 for Ram-Lak); by 180 views the sharp **Ram-Lak** is best (0.037 vs 0.055 for Hann). The ranking crosses over near 45–60 views. This is the expected bias–variance behaviour: at sparse angles, high-frequency streak noise dominates and apodisation (which attenuates high frequencies) reduces error; as sampling becomes dense and noise falls, resolution dominates and the un-tapered ramp wins.

### 4.3 Analytic vs iterative under photon noise (64×64, I₀=3×10⁴)

Table 3 and Figure 3 compare the four reconstructors under Poisson photon noise. **ART attains the lowest RMSE at every dose** — its non-negativity constraint and row-action updates act as an implicit regulariser. **DMR (unconstrained least squares) is unstable:** at 60 views its RMSE spikes to 0.611, roughly an order of magnitude worse than the others. With 64 detectors × 60 views the system has 3,840 equations for 4,096 unknowns — nearly square — while the 30-view (under-determined, minimum-norm) and 90-view (over-determined) cases are far more stable. The standard reading is that conditioning is worst at the near-square point; §4.4 measures it, and the measurement does not support that reading in its naive form. **SIRT is stable but conservative** (≈0.075), more robust than DMR and slightly behind ART. FBP is a stable but streak-limited baseline (≈0.09).

| 30 | 0.099 | 0.151 | **0.069** | 0.083 |
| 60 | 0.089 | **0.611** | **0.053** | 0.076 |
| 90 | 0.087 | 0.090 | **0.050** | 0.075 |

### 4.4 Measuring the conditioning that §4.3 only asserted

The account above is the standard one, and until now it was the *only* thing in this study that had never been measured. To close that gap we compute the singular value decomposition of the very same system matrices §4.3 uses — `recon_cond.py` calls `recon.make_theta` and `recon.build_system_matrix` on the identical arguments, so the matrices are bit-for-bit the ones the reconstructions ran on.

| Views | Equations × unknowns | cond₂(A) | Numerical rank | Retained by `lstsq` | σ at cutoff | Noise gain 1/σ_k | Discarded directions | DMR RMSE |
|------:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 30 | 1,920 × 4,096 | **1.48 × 10¹⁷** | 1,919 | 1,919 | 0.0340 | 29.5 | 2,177 | 0.151 |
| 60 | 3,840 × 4,096 | 8.10 × 10¹⁵ | 3,839 | 3,707 | 0.0289 | **34.6** | 389 | **0.611** |
| 90 | 5,760 × 4,096 | 3.36 × 10³ | 4,096 | 4,081 | 0.0517 | 19.3 | 15 | 0.090 |

*Table 4. Measured conditioning of the §4.3 system matrices. `numpy.linalg.lstsq(rcond=None)` on a float32 `A` truncates singular values below `max(m,n)·ε_float32·σ_max`, so the quantity governing noise amplification is not σ_min but the smallest **retained** singular value σ_k. Reproduce with `python experiments/recon_cond.py` → `results/exp_c_conditioning.csv`.*

Two things follow. The first contradicts what §4.3 has been asserting; the second is weaker than we would like, and deliberately so.

**The 2-norm condition number is not worst at 60 views — it is worst at 30.** At 1.48 × 10¹⁷ against 8.10 × 10¹⁵, the sparse case is more than an order of magnitude worse by the textbook measure, because `A` is far more rank-deficient there (rank 1,919 of 4,096). The naive statement "the condition number peaks near the square regime" is false for these matrices, and this study asserted it for three revisions without checking.

**Of the quantities measured here, the only one that is non-monotone in view count — peaking at 60, as the RMSE does — is the conditioning of the subspace least squares actually inverts.** Because `lstsq` discards singular directions below its cutoff, the operative gain on the worst retained direction is `‖A⁺‖₂ = 1/σ_k`, and that goes 29.5 → **34.6** → 19.3. Every other column of Table 4 moves monotonically with view count, including the count of discarded directions (2,177 → 389 → 15, i.e. the implicit truncated-SVD regularisation weakens monotonically as sampling gets denser). So a conditioning account survives measurement, but only when stated about the retained spectrum rather than about σ_min.

**What this does *not* license is any quantitative attribution of the spike.** An earlier revision of this section divided the RMSE ratios by the `1/σ_k` ratios and reported the remainder — "a factor of ~3.5 unexplained". That arithmetic is invalid and is withdrawn. `‖A⁺‖₂` is a worst-case operator-norm bound on the amplification of a perturbation aligned with one singular direction; the reconstruction RMSE is an end-to-end quantity in which the noise is Poisson (so its covariance changes with view count, and with the sinogram values themselves), the discarded directions contribute truncation *bias* rather than amplified variance, and the solution is clipped to `[0, 1]` before the error is taken in-circle. A ratio of the bound and a ratio of the outcome are not commensurable, and their quotient is not a residual. Decomposing the spike properly would need the noise covariance propagated through `A⁺` — for instance `Σᵢ (uᵢᵀδp)²/σᵢ²` with the actual per-ray variances — plus a separate accounting of truncation bias and clipping. **That was not done here, so this study reports the two measured facts above and makes no claim about how much of the 0.611 either one explains.**

## 5. Discussion

The three results form one coherent message: **the right reconstruction choice depends on the dose regime.** In the abundant-dose regime, FBP with the sharp Ram-Lak filter is accurate and fast, and extra views add little. As dose falls — whether by sparser sampling or lower photon counts — the picture changes twice over. First, within FBP, the filter optimum shifts toward apodisation, because suppressing amplified high-frequency noise matters more than preserving resolution (§4.2). Second, when noise is genuinely photon-limited, unregularised inversion is the wrong tool: its error is governed by the conditioning of the projection matrix — though not in the way this study first assumed, since the measured 2-norm condition number is worst at the *sparsest* sampling, and what peaks near the square regime is the conditioning of the subspace least squares retains, together with the collapse of the implicit truncated-SVD regularisation that carried the sparse case (§4.4). Constrained iteration (ART) side-steps this by injecting prior information (non-negativity) and never explicitly inverting `A`, which is why it dominates across the noisy doses tested. These observations reproduce, in miniature and end-to-end, the rationale behind the field's move toward iterative and model-based reconstruction for low-dose CT [3].

The DMR spike is worth dwelling on because it is a common source of confusion, and because measuring it changed our own explanation. The robust part of the observation is that a *larger* number of views (90) yields a *more* stable least-squares solution than a near-square system (60), even though both are "more data" than the sparse case: data quantity alone does not determine stability, the geometry of the linear system does. The part we had wrong is which number expresses that geometry. It is not σ_min — by that measure the 30-view system is the worst of the three — but the smallest singular value the solver actually keeps. That distinction only became visible once the SVD was computed rather than cited. How *much* of the spike that accounts for is a separate question this study does not answer: it would require propagating the Poisson noise covariance through `A⁺` and separating truncation bias and clipping from amplified variance, none of which is done here (§4.4).

## 6. Limitations

This is a deliberately small, transparent study. (i) It uses a single 2-D analytic phantom; real anatomy, 3-D geometry, beam hardening, and scatter are not modelled. (ii) The matrix methods are limited to 64×64 by the cost of forming `A`, so absolute numbers in §4.3 are not directly comparable to the 256×256 FBP results in §4.1–4.2. (iii) The photon-noise model is a simplified monoenergetic Beer–Lambert model with a single `I₀`; electronic noise and bowtie filtration are omitted, so it should not be read as realistic dose simulation. (iv) ART/SIRT iteration counts were fixed rather than tuned per dose, so every statement about ART's and SIRT's relative standing holds **under the tested iteration counts** and is not established for tuned ones. (v) §4.4 measures the conditioning of the three §4.3 systems and reports a qualitative agreement, but attempts no quantitative decomposition of the DMR spike — the noise covariance is not propagated, and truncation bias and clipping are not separated from amplified variance. These choices favour reproducibility and interpretability over realism. We previously claimed here that none of them affected the qualitative conclusions; that claim is withdrawn — it was never tested, and §4.4 is a case of a qualitative conclusion that measurement did partly overturn.

## 7. Conclusion

Using one open pipeline and a standard phantom, we quantified three dose-dependent reconstruction tradeoffs: FBP quality saturates beyond ≈180 views; the optimal FBP filter inverts from apodising (sparse) to sharp (dense) with a 45–60-view crossover; and under photon noise, constrained iteration (ART) is most robust — under the fixed iteration counts tested — while unregularised least-squares inversion destabilises near the square-system regime, for reasons that direct measurement of the system matrices supports only in part. The study regenerates from seeded scripts and uses no patient data, making it suitable as a teaching artefact and as a baseline for further work on sparse-view and low-dose reconstruction.

## Reproducibility

All figures and tables are regenerated by:

```bash
conda activate dicom_gui
pip install -r experiments/requirements-experiments.txt
python experiments/recon_study.py a b c
python experiments/recon_cond.py
```

Outputs are written to `experiments/results/` (PNG figures + CSV data). The reconstruction routines under test are in `recon.py`; the study harness is `experiments/recon_study.py`, and `experiments/recon_cond.py` produces Table 4 by taking the SVD of the same system matrices §4.3 reconstructs from (it reuses `recon.build_system_matrix`, so it hits the on-disk matrix cache rather than rebuilding). Both are deterministic: phantom and photon noise are seeded, and the conditioning measurement has no random component at all. Environment: Python 3.10, NumPy 2.2, SciPy 1.15, scikit-image 0.25.
